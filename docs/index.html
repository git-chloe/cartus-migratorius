01 / The research question

Where do the carts go?

I set out to understand when and where abandoned shopping carts were reported in Ottawa—and ended up investigating missing locations, misleading closure dates, recurring addresses, unusual isolation and the surprising power of two nearby streetside witnesses.

The raw source is municipal service requests. Nothing here tracks the same physical cart, proves a journey or identifies a retailer of origin. That distinction became the central rule of the study.

05 / The Woodroffe affair

The brothers who changed the verdict.

Two Park Maintenance reporting addresses were just 225.3 metres apart, and their estimated episode starts were two days apart.

Together they generated eight April–May reports but only two estimated episodes under the study’s 21-day rule. Removing them in a sensitivity check did not erase the spring concentration—but it did change its size and interpretation.

A space–time coincidence is not proof of a common cart or shared incident.

07 / What the evidence cannot say

The important fine print.

A good field guide should say where the evidence ends.

01 / OBSERVATION

Reports ≠ carts

311 requests do not identify or track individual shopping carts. Repeat requests may describe the same situation—or different ones.

02 / MISSINGNESS

164 locations absent

Missing coordinates cluster within service families. Any map omits 40% of the reported population.

03 / SPATIAL MODELS

Proximity ≠ provenance

Retailer candidates came from a later places archive. Distances are straight-line; origins and actual travel routes are unknown.
